Data Visual Vocabulary Challenge

The Financial Times built a guide called the Visual Vocabulary that sorts charts into nine families, grouped by the kind of relationship you want to show. Here is the challenge: pick a dataset you care about and create one chart in each of the nine families. A tip and a small example sit in every card. Check each one off as you finish.

0 of 9 built
01

Deviation

Show how values rise above or fall below a fixed reference point, usually zero, a target, or an average.

Try: diverging bar, diverging stacked bar, surplus and deficit line.

Tip. Anchor everything to a clear zero line so readers can see at a glance what sits above it and what sits below.

02

Correlation

Show the relationship between two or more variables, and whether they move together.

Try: scatterplot, bubble chart, connected scatterplot.

Tip. A pattern is not proof of cause. Let the chart suggest the link, and explain the limits in your words.

03

Ranking

Use when an item's place in an ordered list matters more than its exact amount.

Try: ordered bar, ordered column, lollipop, dot plot.

Tip. Sort the bars by value and label them directly, so the order itself does the explaining.

04

Distribution

Show the values in a dataset and how often each one occurs. The shape reveals spread and outliers.

Try: histogram, box plot, violin plot, population pyramid.

Tip. Choose a sensible bin width. Too many bins looks noisy, too few hides the real shape.

05

Change over Time

Give a sense of a trend, showing how a value moves across a period.

Try: line chart, area chart, column over time, slope chart.

Tip. Keep the time steps evenly spaced. If you use bars or area, start the value axis at zero.

06

Magnitude

Compare sizes or amounts between things, either in absolute numbers or relative to each other.

Try: column chart, bar chart, paired column, bullet chart.

Tip. Compare lengths, not areas. People judge the length of a bar far more accurately than the size of a circle.

07

Part-to-whole

Show how a single total breaks down into the pieces that make it up.

Try: stacked bar, pie, donut, treemap, waterfall.

Tip. Use this only when the parts truly add up to one whole, and keep the number of pieces small.

08

Spatial

Use when the location itself is the point, and the pattern lives on a map.

Try: choropleth map, proportional symbol map, dot density, cartogram.

Tip. Reach for a map only when place matters. If it does not, a plain bar chart is often clearer.

09

Flow

Show movement or volume passing between two or more states, places, or stages.

Try: Sankey diagram, chord diagram, network, waterfall.

Tip. Show direction and volume together, and limit the number of paths so the picture stays readable.

The nine categories come from the Financial Times Visual Vocabulary, created by the FT visual journalism team and inspired by the Graphic Continuum. You can see the original guide and example files on the FT chart-doctor page. Your progress on this challenge is saved in your browser on this device.
